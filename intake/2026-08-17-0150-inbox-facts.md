# 2026-08-17: 0150-message-inbox — facts the track cannot proceed without (intake)

**Status**: open
**Owner**: app owner (a–h, j–k), plus legal/procurement (l) and the contract owners (c)

Track [0150-message-inbox](../tracks/0150-message-inbox/research-plan.md) asks whether selected
inbound MQTT streams should get a durable inbox. Items **a–d** gate the survey rather than
colour it: under one set of answers the recommendation is **skip** and no storage candidate
needs comparing. Partial answers are fine, and "I don't know" is itself an answer worth
recording — several of these are observations nobody has had reason to make yet.

## a — MQTT session mode, and is the clientId stable?

Does the client connect with `clean: true` (mqtt.js's default) or `clean: false`? And is the
clientId stable across reloads and across tabs, or mqtt.js's per-instance random default?

This is the track's null hypothesis. Under a clean session the broker keeps no session state,
so there is no cross-reload QoS 1 redelivery at all — redelivery happens only within a live
connection, and that is already handled by the in-memory dedup guard accepted in D-0015 and the
ingress LRU accepted in D-0016. If `clean: true` holds and no other duplicate source exists
(see **e**, **h**), the durable half of this track has no traffic to act on. 0060 recorded
`clean: true` as **assumption A-5**, never as a confirmed fact.

→ Resolution: confirms or defeats the null hypothesis → updates facts/app-profile.md

**2026-08-17 update — ANSWERED, and the null hypothesis is defeated.** The user: the session
runs **`clean: false` with a persisted clientId**. Recorded in facts/app-profile.md. The track
is live; skip is no longer its default verdict. Four consequences, propagated: 0060's A-5 and
0070's A-6 are falsified and both accepted reports now carry dated in-place corrections; the
mqtt.js #909 duplicate history A-5 excluded is back in scope; a reconnect is no longer
automatically a gap for QoS 1 (0070's rule is now over-conservative there, safe but costly);
and `resubscribe: true` is redundant against a retained session while still re-triggering
retained delivery on every reconnect.

One consequence was not anticipated by this item and is now the plan's centre of gravity.
mqtt.js sends the PUBACK **from inside the `handleMessage` callback**, and the default handler
completes immediately — so the acknowledgement precedes any async work the app starts. A
persistent session therefore gives the *appearance* of at-least-once delivery across reloads
while a crash between acknowledgement and effect loses the message permanently and silently.
Losing a message is worse than applying one twice. Deferring the ack is the fix, and it
collides head-on with 0060 Key question 5's "never do slow work in `handleMessage`". That
collision is plan question 2.

## b — Which streams, and are their effects idempotent?

The charter says "certain arbitrary messages". Which topics or streams are those, roughly what
share of the ~50 msg/s peak do they carry, and — the question that actually decides the design
— **are their effects idempotent?**

An effect that replaces or invalidates (last-write-wins, "refetch this entity") is already
safe under redelivery and needs no inbox. An effect that accumulates — appends to a list,
increments a counter, enqueues work, fires a notification, triggers a REST write — is the only
kind that an inbox protects. If every stream is in the first category, the answer is skip.

→ Resolution: scopes the track, or ends it → updates facts/app-profile.md

**2026-08-18 — this is now the track's primary input.** The user has scoped the work: IndexedDB
round trips are wanted **only for messages on certain topics**, not for all traffic. That makes
this item three things at once — the selection rule that goes on 0060's policy row, the message
rate every cost estimate is built on (the durable topics' share, not the ~50/s aggregate), and,
paired with the idempotence question, the measure of how much design is needed at all. A list
of topic filters plus a rough share of traffic is the single most useful answer available to
this track.

One caution worth recording here, because it is easy to assume otherwise: per-topic scoping
limits how *often* the durable path runs, not what it costs when it does. `handleMessage` is
one client-wide serialized hook, so a durable write inside it blocks every other topic too.
Selection reduces frequency, not blast radius.

**2026-08-18 update — partially answered.** The user: **~6 of roughly 40 topics**, at a
**ceiling of ~5 msg/s** (against the ~50 msg/s aggregate). Recorded in facts/app-profile.md,
and it settles the design's hardest question in the simple direction — at 5 msg/s, per-message
acknowledgement deferral runs at a 0.8% duty cycle nominally and 10% pessimistically, so
batching is an optimization rather than a necessity and the second-connection option is not
needed. It also makes dedup-key retention derivable from the broker's session-expiry interval
rather than chosen, at roughly 1.2 MB of keys per hour of expiry.

**Still needed on this item**, and now the remaining gate:

- **Who publishes to those 6 topics — MQTT clients, or JMS/OpenWire/STOMP/AMQP/Camel producers?**
  Added 2026-08-18 and now the **first** question in this whole intake file. ActiveMQ Classic
  inverts the JMS-persistence-to-QoS mapping (AMQ-7045, open since 2018, unchanged in 6.3.1), so
  a message from a non-MQTT producer reaches the browser at QoS 0 — there is no acknowledgement
  to defer, the subscription is not durable, and nothing is retained while the tab is closed. In
  a shop running ActiveMQ Classic, JMS producers are the normal case. If that is what these 6
  topics have, the inbox can still deduplicate but cannot prevent loss, and the track should say
  so plainly rather than specify a mechanism that never engages. Everything else in this file is
  downstream of this answer.
- **Which 6 topics** — the filters that go on the policy rows. A list is the deliverable. Audit
  them for literal `.` characters while you are there: ActiveMQ transposes `/`↔`.`, `#`↔`>` and
  `+`↔`*` in both directions, so `a.b/c` and `a/b/c` collide on the same destination.
- **Are their effects idempotent?** An effect that replaces or invalidates is already safe under
  redelivery; only accumulating effects (append, increment, enqueue, notify, trigger a REST
  write) need the inbox. If some of the 6 are idempotent, the set shrinks further.
- **Are all 6 QoS 1?** A durable row at QoS 0 is nonsense — no redelivery to deduplicate, no
  acknowledgement to defer, and the broker does not queue it across a disconnection. Plan
  question 17 proposes making that combination unrepresentable, which only works if the answer
  here is yes.
- **Rough payload size on those topics.** Retention sizing above counts keys only; storing
  effects as well scales with payload.

## c — Is there a stable per-message identifier today, and can one be added?

Does any payload already carry a field that is unique and stable per logical event — a message
id, sequence number, or server-assigned identifier? MQTT itself supplies none: the Packet
Identifier is a per-session slot released for reuse once acknowledged, so it cannot survive a
reload, and QoS 0 has no identifier at all.

If no such field exists, the dedup key must be a hash of the canonicalized payload, which is
strictly worse (see **e**) — so the alternative is a contract change. Note this is a **sibling
of D-0019, not the same request**: D-0019 asks for a monotonic *ordering* stamp (which of two
messages is fresher), and this asks for an *identity* key (have I seen this exact message).
They answer different questions and one does not imply the other. But they go to the same
contract owners, so asking together is one conversation instead of two.

→ Resolution: selects the dedup-key branch → may extend the D-0019 contract request

## d — Where do effects land today — durable storage, or memory only?

The inbox pattern requires the identity write and the effect write to share one transaction. If
message effects are applied only to in-memory Zustand/xstate state and never persisted, **there
is nothing for the key write to be atomic with**, and the pattern collapses to a durable
seen-set — a much smaller thing that should not be called an inbox. Does the app persist any
state to IndexedDB or localStorage today? If so, under what database name and schema version,
and who owns migrations?

→ Resolution: fixes the transaction scope → updates facts/app-profile.md

## e — Retained messages and overlapping topic filters

Two MQTT behaviours produce deliveries that are byte-identical to an already-processed message
but are **not** duplicates, and a content-hash dedup key would silently swallow both:

1. **Retained messages.** [MQTT-3.3.1-6] requires the broker to send the last retained message
   on every new subscription, and mqtt.js defaults `resubscribe: true` — so every reconnect
   re-triggers it. Suppressing this is actively harmful: 0060 treats every reconnect as an
   unrecoverable gap, and the retained message is the repair.
2. **Overlapping filters.** MQTT 3.1.1 §3.3.5 permits one copy per matching subscription where
   a client's filters overlap. Those copies are intended deliveries.

Do any subscribed topics carry retained messages? Does the client subscribe to overlapping
filters (a wildcard plus a more specific filter that both match)?

→ Resolution: constrains the dedup key → updates facts/app-profile.md

## f — Multiple tabs, and the scope of the persisted clientId

**2026-08-18 — this is now the most urgent open question in this file, and one branch of it is a
production defect that has nothing to do with the inbox.** The broker is ActiveMQ Classic with
**`allowLinkStealing` enabled on the `wss` connector**, which overrides ActiveMQ's own default
of rejecting a duplicate clientId. So a second connection presenting the same clientId
**disconnects the first**.

If the persisted clientId is shared across tabs, that plus mqtt.js's 1000 ms `reconnectPeriod`
is a **steal war**: tab B evicts tab A, A reconnects and steals back, and so on. 0060's boundary
caps reconnection at `maxAttempts` (default 10) before declaring `degraded`, so the predicted
symptom is **both tabs going `degraded` and silently stopping** rather than flapping forever.
That prediction is falsifiable in about a minute — open the app in two tabs and watch. If anyone
has already reported "opening a second tab kills the first" or "the app goes dead with two
windows open", the mechanism is identified and it should be fixed regardless of what happens to
track 0150.

**2026-08-18, later still — CORRECTED, and the question changes shape.** The clientId is
**device-scoped**, with a single connection using it at a time. An earlier reading of "a single
unique client id per tab" as *per-tab minting* was wrong; this repo's records are corrected in
place. Two consequences.

First, the orphan liability is real but at **device** rate, not tab rate — one set of 6 durable
subscriptions per device, reused across tabs and reloads, orphaned only when a device never
returns (decommissioned machine, cleared profile, departed user). The mechanisms below are
unchanged and still worth reading; only the arrival rate is wrong. Note the severity of a single
orphan is **not** reduced: one never-acking subscriber still pins every journal file written
since it was created, so this still needs `offlineDurableSubscriberTimeout`.

Second, **the remaining question is what enforces "one at a time"**. It is stated as intent —
"there *should* only be a single client connecting" — and the whole architecture rests on it,
because with link stealing enabled two tabs on one device present the same clientId and produce
the steal war. So:

- **Is single-connection enforced in code today** — a leader election, a SharedWorker, a
  single-tab guard — or is it a convention that holds because users happen not to open two tabs?
- If enforced, **what happens when the owning tab crashes, is frozen by Chromium's Energy Saver,
  or is backgrounded past the throttling threshold?** Those are the cases where an election that
  looks correct in a demo fails in the field.

If nothing enforces it, the plan's recommendation is Web Locks leader election — not as a repair,
but as the mechanism that makes an already-assumed invariant true.

**2026-08-18, further — the roster is STATIC**: multiple clients, a fixed number, each with a
unique persistent clientId. That is the most favourable shape and it turns the liability from
unbounded accrual into a bounded set plus a hygiene problem. It also **withdraws the
`offlineDurableSubscriberTimeout` recommendation**, because reaping a legitimately-absent client
silently discards exactly the backlog this work exists to preserve. Four questions replace it,
all cheap:

- **How large is the roster, and how often does it change?** Retirements, replacements and
  reimages are the only events that create permanent orphans now.
- **What is the longest *legitimate* offline window for a client** — overnight, a weekend, a
  holiday shutdown, a spare unit sitting in storage? If any client can be offline for an
  unbounded period, no safe timeout exists at all and a decommissioning procedure is the only
  correct tool. If the maximum is knowable, storage is computable: roster size × that window ×
  message rate.
- **Is there a decommissioning procedure today?** Retiring a client should explicitly remove its
  durable subscriptions — an UNSUBSCRIBE, one `clean: true` connect under that clientId, or
  `BrokerView.destroyDurableSubscriber` over JMX. If none exists, every past retirement is
  already an orphan, and the count of `getInactiveDurableTopicSubscribers()` minus the number of
  currently-offline roster members gives the backlog to clean up.
- **What happens when a NEW client is provisioned?** Every subscription is created
  `retroactive(true)`, so a new clientId's starting position may be the union of sequences still
  outstanding for every other durable subscriber on those topics — a replacement unit would take
  the whole fleet's undrained backlog as one burst. This is a source-level inference that needs
  empirical confirmation, and it is a good spike candidate.

The paragraph below was written against the per-tab reading and is left as the record. Verified in `apache/activemq` source, with three corrections
that all made it worse: one never-acking orphan pins every journal file written since it was
created; the pinning is **not confined to the 6 topics**, because journal files are shared by
all destinations and garbage collection is all-or-nothing per file, so retained bytes track the
broker's *total* persistent write volume; and there is no quiet period, because
`canOptimizeOutPersistence()` is `durableSubscribers.size() == 0` and that is never true again
once a tab has connected. Orphan count separately drives heap. The endgame is broker-wide:
persistent producers block indefinitely in `waitForSpace()` at the store high water mark.

**This needs raising with whoever owns the broker now, not at the go gate**, because it is
probably already accruing. Two authorities to hand them: MQTT 3.1.1 §3.1.3.1's non-normative
comment that random clientIds "should be actively discouraged when the CleanSession is set to
0", and ActiveMQ's own "Manage durable subscribers" page, which names this exact failure. One
diagnosis trap to pass on: **do not rank offenders by pending count** —
`getPendingQueueSize()` returns 0 for inactive subscriptions, so JMX and the web console both
read zero for precisely the orphans being hunted. Use the length of
`BrokerViewMBean.getInactiveDurableTopicSubscribers()` (should be flat, will climb by 6 per
tab), per-topic `getStoreMessageSize()`, and the KahaDB `db-*.log` file count.

The original framing follows. With `clean: false` and a *persisted* clientId, the scope of that
id decides which of two failure modes the app has today:

- **Shared across tabs** (persisted in `localStorage` or similar): with link stealing enabled,
  two tabs evict each other in a reconnect loop, each takeover producing a reconnect the other
  treats as a gap, until 0060's give-up policy parks both in `degraded`. If this is the
  configuration and multi-tab is a supported scenario, it is a defect that exists independently
  of any inbox work.
- **Per-tab** (persisted in `sessionStorage`, or derived per connection): every tab ever opened
  leaves an abandoned persistent session on the broker, which keeps queueing QoS 1 messages
  against subscriptions no one will ever read, until a session-expiry policy reaps it. That is
  a broker-side resource question and needs the expiry setting.

Which is it — and is there a session-expiry interval configured on the broker?

Then the original question: can one user have several tabs open against the same broker, and is
state expected to be shared or independent between them? Tabs share one IndexedDB but hold
independent MQTT connections, so the same logical message can arrive twice into one shared
store.

0060 dismissed cross-tab coordination on **assumption A-13** ("no multi-tab coordination
requirement exists") and explicitly flagged it as reopening if tabs share a connection. Durable
storage is cross-tab by construction where the current in-memory dedup is not, so this track
reopens the question regardless of A-13's fate. This overlaps the still-open multi-tab item in
the Wave 2 intake; answering it here settles both.

→ Resolution: decides whether leader election is in scope → updates facts/app-profile.md

## h — Broker topology and protocol version

Is the broker clustered, bridged, or fronted by shared subscriptions? Each can duplicate a
message independently of QoS, which changes what the dedup key must be stable across. (Item a
already defeated the null hypothesis, so this no longer decides whether the track runs — but a
clustered or bridged broker widens what the key must be stable across, and a clustered broker
also raises whether the persistent session in item a is pinned to one node.)

Three broker settings now travel with this:

- The **session-expiry interval** — how long the broker holds an offline session's queue.
- The **per-session queue depth limit**. With the interval above, this sizes the reconnect
  replay burst that plan question 5 measures against 0060's bounded delivery queue, which sheds
  *oldest* at a default of 256.
- The **in-flight window** — the broker's max-inflight setting (3.1.1 has no protocol-level
  `Receive Maximum`). If acknowledgements are held back, the broker stops sending once that
  window fills. That is correct backpressure rather than a fault, and it is what makes deferral
  safe, but it caps how long a commit may take.

**2026-08-18 update — protocol version answered, broker product is now the ask.** The user:
**MQTT 3.1.1 (protocolVersion 4)**, no session-expiry interval, **keepalive 30 s**. Recorded in
facts/app-profile.md; the protocol consequences are written up in the plan.

The remaining question on this item is narrower and much more valuable than the three settings
above: **which broker product and version?** "Undefined" settings are defaults in force, and
the defaults were measured across the mainstream brokers — they *invert* rather than differ,
so four separate pieces of design logic branch on the answer:

| | queue depth | in-flight | session expiry | drops | retransmits while connected |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mosquitto | 1000 | 20 | never | newest | no (since 1.5) |
| EMQX | 1000 | 32 | 2 h | **oldest** | no (v5; v4 was 30 s) |
| HiveMQ CE | 1000 | 50 | never | newest | unverified |
| VerneMQ | 1000 + 1000 | 20 | never | newest | **yes, 20 s** |
| AWS IoT | unpublished | 100 (fixed) | 1 h | newest | **yes, up to 1 h** |

Naming the product settles all of it.

**2026-08-18 update — ANSWERED: Apache ActiveMQ Classic. The table above is superseded.** It is
kept in place as the record of why the question mattered; none of those rows apply, because
Classic is a JMS broker with an MQTT transport rather than a native MQTT broker. The findings
are written up in the plan and summarised in facts/app-profile.md.

**One answer displaced the whole question, and it is now item b's first ask**: ActiveMQ Classic
maps JMS persistence to MQTT QoS with the ternary inverted, so messages from any non-MQTT
producer (JMS/OpenWire, STOMP, AMQP, Camel) arrive at the browser at **QoS 0** — no PUBACK to
defer, no durable subscription, no offline retention. That is AMQ-7045, open since 2018.

**Three things still needed on this item**, each cheap to answer and each changing something:

- **The ActiveMQ version.** It is a security floor, not bookkeeping: CVE-2026-40046's fix was
  missed for all 6.0.0+ until 6.2.4, and a `wss://` connector decodes MQTT with the same class
  as an `mqtt://` one, so the advisory's "not enabling mqtt transport connectors are not
  impacted" does **not** exempt this deployment. 6.1.x and 6.2.0–6.2.3 are vulnerable on a
  browser-facing endpoint.
- ~~**The transport connector URI**, verbatim, for `allowLinkStealing`.~~ **Answered
  2026-08-18: link stealing is enabled on the `wss` connector, and the ruling is to KEEP it.**
  The operator offered to disable it; declining, for reasons written up in the plan — it cannot
  help with unique-per-tab clientIds, it would add 14–60 s of outage per ungraceful drop, it
  would violate [MQTT-3.1.4-2], and it would re-arm a second "Durable consumer is in use" gate.
  One connector question does remain: **is `transport.useInactivityMonitor` set to false?** If
  so the read checker is never created, ghosts persist until TCP-level failure, and the
  arithmetic above changes completely.

- **Three client-side settings to check** (added 2026-08-18; none decidable from here under
  D-0004, and the first two may be live defects):
  1. **Does the transport boundary set `reconnectPeriod: 0`?** Its own exponential backoff with
     a give-up policy implies yes, since mqtt.js's native reconnect is a fixed interval. If so,
     mqtt.js's resubscribe bookkeeping is gated off (`_resubscribeTopics` is only populated when
     `reconnectPeriod > 0`) and `options.resubscribe` is a **no-op**. That is normally invisible
     — but ActiveMQ does **not** restore QoS-0 subscriptions on a `clean: false` reconnect, so
     unless the boundary re-subscribes explicitly, the app goes deaf on the 34 QoS-0 topics after
     its first reconnect. Worth checking today.
  2. **Is a will message configured?** *Sharpened 2026-08-18: if yes, this is a live bug.*
     `MQTTSocket.onWebSocketClose` tests a `receivedDisconnect` flag that is set only when a real
     DISCONNECT frame arrives, and mqtt.js sends none on page unload — so on every ordinary tab
     close ActiveMQ calls `onTransportError()`, which **publishes the will**, and only then
     synthesises the DISCONNECT that releases the clientId. Confirmed identical on `main` and
     `activemq-5.18.x`. So a configured will currently fires on every close and refresh rather
     than on genuine failures. This is the one thing an explicit page-hide disconnect would
     actually fix ([MQTT-3.14.4-3]); if no will is configured, that proposal has no MQTT-side
     value at all. One grep decides it.
  3. **Adopt `reconnectOnConnackError: true`** regardless of the link-stealing decision. It has
     existed since mqtt.js 5.10.3, is absent from `defaultConnectOptions` so currently falsy, and
     without it recovery from any refused CONNACK depends on the broker closing the socket rather
     than on the client. It costs nothing when connections are accepted.
- **`offlineDurableSubscriberTimeout`**, and who owns it. Default `-1` means the reaper thread
  is never constructed, so abandoned durable subscriptions accrete forever; and because
  `sendFailIfNoSpace` defaults false with no timeout, a full store blocks **every publisher**
  indefinitely. That combination turns one forgotten browser profile into a broker-wide
  availability incident, which makes this a prerequisite to shipping rather than tuning — and
  puts whoever owns broker availability in this track's audience.

  **Remediation is two changes, not one** (added 2026-08-18). Switching to `clean: true` stops
  *new* leakage but reclaims nothing already accumulated: `deleteDurableSubs` only touches the
  subscriptions of the clientId connecting at that moment, and with a unique id per tab a new tab
  never sees the orphans of dead ones. So the existing backlog comes back only via
  `offlineDurableSubscriberTimeout` or a JMX sweep, and new leakage stops only via the clientId
  or `cleanSession` change. Either alone leaves it half-solved.
- **Is `transport.useInactivityMonitor` set to false on the connector?** If so the read checker is
  never created and ghost connections persist until TCP-level failure rather than 45–60 s, which
  invalidates the reconnect arithmetic behind the link-stealing ruling.

A separate observation, independent of this track and worth checking on its own: keepalive 30 s
means a 45 s server-side deadline, while Chromium throttles timers in a tab hidden more than
five minutes to about once a minute, with WebSocket not on the exemption list. That combination
plausibly disconnects backgrounded tabs, which under `clean: false` then resume and replay. If
that is happening today, raising keepalive above ~120 s is the mitigation.

Separately: which MQTT protocol version does the client negotiate, 3.1.1 or 5.0? This decides
two things — whether MQTT 5 User Properties and Correlation Data are available as a place to
carry the identifier in **c**, and which acknowledgement-deferral seam exists at all
(`customHandleAcks` is silently a no-op below protocol version 5; `manualAcks` does not exist
in mqtt.js at any version).

## i — What durability is actually wanted, and can the Firefox floor move?

Must the inbox survive an **OS crash or power loss**, or is a tab crash / page reload the only
failure it needs to survive? This is not a detail. IndexedDB gives genuine all-or-nothing commit
across object stores on both target engines, but it does **not** give a flush guarantee: Firefox
has been relaxed since version 40 and exposes no `durability` option below 126 — the stated
floor is ~124 — and Chromium's default is now relaxed too. Chrome's own documentation states
that even `strict` "does not ensure that changes are actually written immediately to disk".

If reload survival is enough, the answer is comfortable. If power-loss safety is required, the
honest report cannot promise it on this matrix, and the follow-up is whether the Firefox floor
can move to 126+.

→ Resolution: fixes what the report may claim → updates facts/app-profile.md

## j — Storage budget, privacy, and the reset path

Four smaller questions that travel together:

- What retention window and storage budget is acceptable for the inbox (records, bytes, or
  days)? Browser eviction is whole-origin and LRU by default; `navigator.storage.persist()`
  exempts an origin but prompts the user in Firefox — is that prompt acceptable?
- Are there **privacy, retention, or encryption constraints** on writing contract payloads (or
  effects derived from them) to durable browser storage? This has never been tested here: 0060
  deliberately kept the quarantine ring in memory and never shipped its contents.
- Must the app work in private/incognito windows, where IndexedDB works but is destroyed at
  session end — degrading silently rather than failing visibly?
- What is the support reset path for local state today? Once the inbox is durable, "reload
  fixes it" stops being true, and clearing site data becomes the only recovery from a poisoned
  store.

→ Resolution: sets retention, quota, and recovery policy → updates facts/app-profile.md

## m — Is a real-browser test lane reachable?

The track's central claim is "a crash between applied and recorded cannot double-apply". The
standard test tool, `fake-indexeddb`, is an in-memory reimplementation of exactly the
auto-commit and durability timing under test — it can confirm the design's shape and never its
guarantee. Both prior spikes reached only Node and happy-dom, never a browser.

Is there an existing e2e/browser harness (or would track 0120 provide one) that could host real
IndexedDB atomicity assertions? If not, the report will say plainly that its central claim is
unverified, which is an acceptable outcome but should be a known one.

→ Resolution: sets the verification bar → may bound what the track can conclude
